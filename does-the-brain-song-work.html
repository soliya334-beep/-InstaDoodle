What The Brain Song Claims to Do

Based on the vendor’s own material, The Brain Song is a digital audio file of about 17 minutes. You listen with headphones, once a day, in a quiet place. The sales pitch says the sound uses specific frequencies, including the gamma range, to guide the brain toward a focused state, and that regular listening supports memory, concentration and mental clarity. It also says the effect of The Brain Song is linked to a protein called BDNF.

Three separate ideas are bundled together in that pitch, and it helps to pull them apart:

  1. Brainwave entrainment. The idea that rhythmic sound can encourage brain activity to follow that rhythm.
  2. Gamma frequencies. The idea that a fast brainwave band linked to attention and memory can be targeted.
  3. BDNF. The idea that listening increases a growth-related protein that helps brain cells connect.

Each one has a different level of support, so we will take them in turn.

Idea One: Brainwave Entrainment

Your brain produces electrical rhythms that scientists group into bands: delta, theta, alpha, beta and gamma. Entrainment is the proposal that if you hear a steady pulse at a certain rate, your brain activity may gradually synchronize with it. It is a tidy idea, and it is the foundation of many focus and relaxation tracks.

The most studied version is the binaural beat. If one ear hears a tone at 200 Hz and the other at 210 Hz, your brain perceives a faint pulse at 10 Hz that does not exist in either ear. Researchers have tested whether listening to these pulses changes attention, memory, mood or pain.

Here is what the larger reviews found:

  • A 2019 meta-analysis published in the journal Psychological Research pooled 22 studies and reported an overall medium effect (g = 0.45) across memory, attention, anxiety and pain. The authors noted the result depended on the frequency used, how long people listened and when the listening happened relative to the task.
  • A 2023 meta-analysis in the same journal looked only at memory and attention. It pooled 15 studies and found a somewhat smaller medium effect (g = 0.40), but its systematic review part found conflicting results, especially for theta and beta frequencies on recall and recognition tasks. The authors themselves called for stronger study designs.

So the fair reading is that binaural beats may help some people on some tasks, the average effect is moderate at best, and the findings are inconsistent. Many of the studies are small, short, and use different sounds and tests, which makes it hard to say what works for whom. Smaller studies also tend to report more positive results than larger ones, a pattern known as publication bias.

It is also worth stating plainly that not every study finds a benefit. Some experiments that measured mood or brain activity directly found no difference between binaural beats and a plain comparison sound.

Idea Two: Gamma Frequencies

Gamma activity, roughly 30 to 100 cycles per second, is associated with attention and memory in brain recordings. Interest in gamma stimulation grew after laboratory work in mice suggested that 40 Hz light and sound stimulation could affect markers related to Alzheimer’s disease. Early human studies of 40 Hz stimulation are now underway, and they generally use carefully designed devices that deliver light and sound together under medical supervision.

That research is exciting, but you should be careful about what it does and does not say. It does not show that a consumer audio file played through headphones produces the same effect. Mouse results do not automatically carry over to people, and clinical device trials are very different from a 17-minute download. In the material we reviewed, the gamma claims for The Brain Song are described in general terms, and we could not find a published trial testing this specific track.

Idea Three: BDNF

BDNF stands for brain-derived neurotrophic factor. It is a protein involved in how neurons grow, form connections and adapt, which is why it features in so many brain-health articles. There is solid evidence that regular aerobic exercise raises BDNF, and good sleep and a healthy diet also matter for brain function.

What we could not find is good evidence that listening to an audio track raises BDNF levels in people in a way that improves memory. Showing that would require measuring BDNF in blood or tissue before and after, with a proper control group. We are not aware of such a published study for The Brain Song. When a sales page says an audio “boosts BDNF”, it is borrowing credibility from real exercise research and applying it to a product that has not been tested the same way.

This does not mean The Brain Song does nothing. It means the BDNF claim, specifically, is unproven.

Why Some People Still Feel a Difference

Even if the sound itself has little direct effect, people can genuinely feel better and concentrate more after a session. There are several honest reasons.

  • Expectation. If you believe a session will sharpen you, you tend to approach your work with more intent, and that alone can improve performance. This is a real effect, not a trick, but it is not caused by the sound.
  • Relaxation. Sitting quietly for 17 minutes with headphones on lowers stimulation. Stress and mental clutter hurt concentration, so a calming break can help afterwards.
  • A cue to start. Many people struggle to begin focused work. A daily audio ritual can act as a clear starting signal, like a runner lacing up shoes.
  • Blocking noise. Headphones reduce interruptions from the environment, which makes concentration easier regardless of what is playing.

These are useful benefits, and none of them requires a special frequency. That is not an insult to The Brain Song. It is simply a clearer way to describe what many users may be experiencing.

What the Evidence Does Not Support

To keep expectations realistic, here are claims that you should treat as unproven for any audio program, including this one.

  • That it prevents or reverses memory loss or dementia.
  • That it works the same way for everyone.
  • That it replaces sleep, exercise, treatment or professional help.
  • That results will be fast, large or permanent.
  • That it has been clinically proven, unless the seller of The Brain Song can point to a published trial of that exact product.

If a page tells you otherwise, that is a reason to be cautious about the whole page.

Safety and Sensible Precautions

Listening to a calm audio track is low-risk for most healthy adults, but there are a few sensible rules.

  • Keep the volume moderate. A common guideline is to stay below about 60 percent of maximum volume on a phone, because long, loud listening can damage hearing.
  • If you have epilepsy or a history of seizures, speak with your doctor before using any rhythmic sound or light stimulation.
  • Do not use it while driving, cycling or operating machinery. Focus audio is for quiet, seated time.
  • If you notice headaches, dizziness or anxiety, stop and seek advice.
  • If you have ongoing memory trouble or sudden confusion, see a doctor. An audio file cannot diagnose or treat a condition.

How Strong Is the Evidence, Really?

Numbers like g = 0.45 sound technical, so here is what they mean in plain terms. The letter g measures how big the difference was between people who heard the sound and people who did not, in units of the spread of scores. A value around 0.4 to 0.5 is usually called a medium effect. In everyday terms, it suggests that an average listener might score somewhat better than an average non-listener, but that the two groups overlap a lot, and many individuals in the listening group would do no better.

There are also reasons to be careful about the quality behind the number.

  • Small studies. Many of the experiments pooled in these reviews had few participants, and small studies are more likely to produce chance results.
  • Different sounds. The experiments used different frequencies, listening lengths and tasks, so averaging them together blurs what works.
  • Hard to blind. People often know whether they are hearing a tone, so expectation can sneak in.
  • Short follow-up. Most measured effects right after listening, not weeks later, so we know little about lasting change.
  • Selective publishing. Studies that find nothing are less likely to be published, which can inflate averages.

This does not make the research worthless. It means the honest label is “promising but uncertain”, and that label applies to The Brain Song at best, since the research is about related sounds and not this exact track.

What a Good Test of This Audio Would Look Like

Imagine how a researcher would prove that The Brain Song works. They would recruit a few hundred adults and split them randomly into two groups. One group would get the real track and the other a similar-sounding track with the key feature removed, and nobody would know which they had. Everyone would complete memory and attention tests before and after several weeks of daily listening, and some would give blood samples if BDNF were being measured. The results would be reported, including any failures, in a peer-reviewed journal.

As far as we could find, no study of that kind exists for The Brain Song. That is not unusual for consumer products, and it is not proof of failure. It simply means the claims rest on plausibility instead of proof, and that your own careful test is the best evidence available to you.

How to Read Health Claims on Any Sales Page

You can protect yourself on this and every similar page with a few habits.

  • Look for the word “may”. Honest claims use it. Phrases such as “clinically proven” with no study named are marketing.
  • Check whether a study is linked. If a page cites research, open it. Does it test the same product, in people, with a control group?
  • Be wary of before-and-after stories. They show the best outcomes and omit everyone else.
  • Notice emotional pressure. Fear of memory loss is powerful, and sellers know it.
  • Ask what The Brain Song would need to prove, then see whether it does.
  • Remember that “natural” and “non-invasive” describe how something works, not whether it works.
