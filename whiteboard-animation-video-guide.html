What a Whiteboard Animation Video Is

A whiteboard animation video, sometimes called a doodle video, shows drawings appearing on screen as if a hand were sketching them while a narrator explains. The format is popular for lessons, product explainers, training and social clips because it turns an abstract idea into a simple visual story, and because the pace is naturally gentle. The viewer watches the drawing form and follows the explanation at the same time.

Until recently, making one meant either learning animation software or hiring a specialist. AI doodle video makers such as InstaDoodle try to shorten that path. You describe a scene in words, InstaDoodle generates drawings, and you arrange them on a timeline with voice and music. Why the Format Works

You will see bold statistics on sales pages about conversion rates and viewer retention. We would not rely on any of them, because they are rarely sourced. The reasons the format tends to work are more modest, and more reliable.

  • It slows information down. Drawing takes time, so each idea gets room to land before the next one arrives.
  • It focuses attention. With few elements on screen, viewers see what matters.
  • It lowers the barrier for abstract topics. A diagram of how something works is easier to follow than a paragraph.
  • It feels approachable. A hand-drawn look is friendly, which suits teaching and onboarding.
  • It is flexible. Drawings work with or without a face on camera.

None of that guarantees results. A badly scripted whiteboard video is as forgettable as any other. The format helps, and the script does the work.

Start With the Viewer, Not the Tool

Before you write anything, answer four questions in one sentence each.

  1. Who is this for? A new employee, a student, a customer who has never heard of your product?
  2. What do they already know? Pitch the InstaDoodle video at that level.
  3. What should they understand or do afterwards? One main takeaway.
  4. Where will they watch it? A lesson page, YouTube, a phone feed, an email?

The last question shapes everything else. A vertical clip for a phone feed needs a faster hook and bigger visuals than a three-minute lesson on a course page.

Write the Script in Pictures

A whiteboard script is a list of things to show, with words attached.

Use the problem, solution, action shape

Most good explainers follow this order: name a problem the viewer recognises, show the idea that solves it, prove it with one example and finish with a clear next step. The shape is simple, and it works for a sixty-second ad and a ten-minute lesson alike.

Aim for short sentences

Most people speak at roughly 130 to 160 words per minute. A 60-second video therefore holds around 150 to 200 words. Short sentences are easier to illustrate and easier to listen to. If a sentence needs two pictures, split it.

Give every sentence a visual

Read your script aloud and, for each sentence, imagine what would appear on screen. If you cannot picture anything, the sentence is abstract. Replace it with a concrete example. Instead of “improve productivity”, say “a person crossing three tasks off a list”.

Cut ruthlessly

Remove introductions, apologies and anything the viewer could skip. A video that ends sooner than expected feels generous.

Seven to ten scenes is plenty for a sixty-second video. If you find yourself planning twenty, you are probably trying to say too much.

Pick a Visual Language and Keep It

Consistency is what makes a whiteboard video feel professional.

  • One drawing style. Do not mix cartoon and realistic characters in the same video.
  • One hand. Keep the same drawing hand throughout.
  • One or two effects. Use the draw effect for main subjects and a simple wipe or fade for labels.
  • Limited text. Put a few words on screen, not sentences. The narrator carries the message.
  • Clear contrast. Dark lines on a light background are easiest to read on phones.

Voice and Sound

A strong voiceover carries a whiteboard video more than any picture does.

  • Record in a quiet room, with soft surfaces nearby to reduce echo.
  • Speak slightly slower than normal conversation, and pause between ideas.
  • Use a conversational tone, as if explaining to a friend.
  • Add gentle background music, set well below the voice.
  • Include captions where possible, since many viewers watch with the sound off.

If you do not want to record your own voice, AI voices exist, and InstaDoodle sells them as an extra. Listen to samples first, because quality and language coverage vary.

Pacing: The Hidden Skill

Pacing is how fast the picture changes relative to the words. Too slow and viewers drift, too fast and they cannot follow.

  1. Each scene should usually last between five and ten seconds.
  2. The picture should appear just before or as the narrator names it.
  3. Let a key drawing finish before moving on.
  4. Vary the rhythm: a quick scene followed by a slower one.
  5. End with a short, still moment so the final message sinks in.

Watch your InstaDoodle video once with the sound off, and ask whether the visuals alone tell the story. Then watch it with your eyes closed and ask whether the audio alone makes sense. Both should mostly work.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Trying to say everything. One video, one idea.
  • Too much on screen. Every extra element competes for attention.
  • Reading a document aloud. Write for the ear, not the eye.
  • No call to action. Tell viewers exactly what to do next.
  • Ignoring the first five seconds. Open with the problem, not your name.
  • No test viewers. Show the InstaDoodle video to two people before publishing and ask what was unclear.
