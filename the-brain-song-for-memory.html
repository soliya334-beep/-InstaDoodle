Can The Brain Song Help Your Memory?

Memory is the first thing people mention when they ask about The Brain Song. Students want to retain more of what they read. Professionals want to stop forgetting names and details. Older adults sometimes worry about slips that feel new.

Here is a straight answer before the details. Nothing in this category can promise a better memory, and we could not find a published trial showing that this specific audio improves memory. What it may offer is a calm, repeatable routine that makes it easier to pay attention, and attention is the doorway to memory. That is a more modest claim, but it is a real one, and it is the one this page is built around.

How Memory Actually Works

You will use any tool better if you understand the basic process. Researchers usually describe memory in three stages.

  1. Encoding. You take in information. If your attention is split, the information is stored weakly or not at all. This is why you can read a page and recall nothing.
  2. Consolidation. The brain strengthens new memories, and a lot of this happens during sleep.
  3. Retrieval. You pull the information back. Practising retrieval strengthens the memory, which is why testing yourself works so well.

Notice where an audio session could plausibly matter. It cannot do your consolidation for you, and it cannot retrieve facts. The only stage it might influence is encoding, through calmer, steadier attention. That is a narrow target, but it is a sensible one.

What the Research Says About Sound and Memory

The closest research concerns binaural beats, a related kind of audio. A 2019 meta-analysis in the journal Psychological Research pooled 22 studies and found a medium average effect across memory, attention, anxiety and pain, noting that the result depended on frequency, listening time and timing. A 2023 meta-analysis looked just at memory and attention across 15 studies and found a medium effect as well, but the review portion of the paper found conflicting results, particularly for theta and beta frequencies on recall and recognition tasks, and the authors asked for better-designed studies.

Put plainly: there is some encouraging evidence for related sounds, the results are inconsistent, and nobody has shown that this particular 17-minute track works. We cover this in depth in does The Brain Song work?

The Habits With the Strongest Evidence

If you care about memory, put these first. They are free, and they have far better evidence than any audio.

  • Sleep. Your brain consolidates what you learned while you sleep. Cutting sleep short to study more often backfires.
  • Retrieval practice. Close the book and write down what you remember. It is uncomfortable, and it works much better than rereading.
  • Spaced repetition. Review material after a day, then a few days, then a week. Spreading practice beats cramming.
  • Aerobic exercise. Regular walking, cycling or running supports brain health and is linked to higher BDNF, the protein that many sales pages mention.
  • Reduced multitasking. Studying with a phone buzzing beside you weakens encoding.
  • Managing stress. Chronic stress interferes with memory, so calming routines can help indirectly.
  • A balanced diet and hydration. Skipping meals and being dehydrated both make concentration harder.

If you are only going to change a few things, change these. Then The Brain Song becomes an optional extra rather than the foundation.

Where an Audio Session Could Fit

Here are four realistic ways The Brain Song might be helpful for memory, none of which requires a miracle.

As a calm-down before study

Seventeen minutes of quiet listening may lower mental clutter before you open a book. If stress usually blocks you, this alone could help encoding.

As a start signal

A routine cue such as headphones and a familiar track tells your brain that focused time is beginning. Many people struggle most with starting, so this is genuinely useful.

As a break from screens

Listening with your eyes closed gives your attention a rest, which can help you concentrate again afterwards.

As a consistent habit

Memory is built by regular practice. If a daily audio ritual helps you show up to study every day, it indirectly helps memory, even if the sound has no special effect.

A Two-Week Memory Test

If you decide to try it, do it properly. This test uses simple recall checks you can repeat, so you do not have to rely on a vague feeling.

What you need

A notebook, a timer and a way to get new material each day. Use similar material each time, such as a short article of 300 words, a list of 12 unrelated words or a page of notes from a course.

The method

1. On days 1 to 3, with no audio, read your material once for five minutes. Wait ten minutes, then write down everything you can recall. Count the correct items.

2. On days 4 to 11, listen to The Brain Song for about 17 minutes first, then do the same read-wait-recall test with new material of the same type and length.

3. On days 12 to 14, skip The Brain Song and repeat the test again.

4. Compare averages: the no-audio days at the start, The Brain Song days, and the no-audio days at the end.

How to read the results

Look for a consistent difference, not one great day. If scores are no different, that suggests The Brain Song is not adding much for you. If they are higher with The Brain Song but also higher at the end without it, the improvement may be practice rather than sound, because you naturally get better at a test the more you do it. Be honest with yourself, and remember that sleep, mood and caffeine all affect scores. Write them in your notes.

Who Should Be Careful

Be thoughtful if any of the following apply.

  • You have noticed new or worsening memory loss. Please see a doctor. Memory changes can have treatable causes, including sleep problems, thyroid issues, low vitamin levels, medication effects and depression.
  • You have epilepsy or a seizure history. Ask your doctor before using rhythmic sound.
  • You are an older adult with real concerns. An audio file is no replacement for a medical assessment.
  • You expect a quick fix. Memory improves slowly, and the best results come from habits.

Memory Tips by Situation

Different memory problems need different tools. Here is how the ideas above apply in four everyday cases, with or without The Brain Song.

Studying for an exam

Space your study sessions over several weeks. After each session, close your notes and write down what you can recall, then check what you missed. Sleep properly the night before, because consolidation matters more than a last-minute push.

Remembering names

When you meet someone, repeat their name aloud, use it in your next sentence and link it to an image or fact about them. Review the names at the end of the day. Names fade quickly without rehearsal.

Learning a language

Short, daily sessions beat long weekly ones. Use flashcards with spaced repetition, say words aloud and use new words in sentences. A calm routine before practice may help you stay focused.

Preparing a presentation

Speak the material aloud instead of reading it silently, because retrieval builds memory. Practise in the room or a similar setting, and sleep well before the event.

The pattern is the same in every case: attend, space, retrieve and rest. A short session with The Brain Song could support the first step, but it cannot do the other three for you.

Why Forgetting Is Normal

Many people believe a good memory means never forgetting. In reality, forgetting is how the brain avoids clutter. A famous line of research from the nineteenth century showed that newly learned material fades quickly at first and then more slowly, a pattern often called the forgetting curve. Reviewing at the right moment flattens it.

This matters when you judge any memory tool. If your recall scores fall after a few days, it does not mean the tool failed. It means you forgot, as everyone does. What matters is whether your scores are better than your own baseline over time, and whether your review habits improve. It also means that you should test recall after a fixed delay each time, such as ten minutes, so the comparison stays fair.

How to Track Progress Over a Month

A month gives a much clearer picture than a week. Here is a simple approach.

  1. Pick one type of material and stick to it, such as 12-word lists or 300-word passages.
  2. Test on the same days each week, such as Monday, Wednesday and Friday.
  3. Record the score, your sleep hours and a note on stress.
  4. Chart your scores on paper or in a spreadsheet at the end of each week.
  5. Compare the first week with the last, looking at averages rather than single days.
  6. Ask whether your habits changed too, since better sleep and more practice can lift scores by themselves.

If your scores rise steadily and you can see no other cause, The Brain Song may be contributing, and you could reasonably keep using it. A Note for Students and Parents

If a teenager or young adult is considering The Brain Song, a few extra points apply. Sleep matters even more at that age, so cutting sleep for late-night study is usually a bad trade. Screens and notifications are the biggest enemy of focus, and removing them beats any product. The sales material describes The Brain Song as aimed at adults, so anyone under 18 should ask a parent or guardian first, and anyone with a health concern should speak to a doctor.

For most students, the best investment is a simple weekly plan: one block of retrieval practice per subject, one review session spread across the week and a fixed bedtime. If a short listening session helps you settle into that plan, good. If not, nothing is lost by skipping it, and nothing should replace the plan itself.
